Business tax records that hold up

The business tax records to keep are the ones made at the time, tied to a clear business purpose and priced at fair market value. Plans that need to be in writing, like an accountable plan, should be signed before the first payment. Keep records generally at least three years after filing, often longer.

Key takeaways

  • Write things down when they happen, not at filing time.
  • Every cost needs a business purpose you can state in one line.
  • Some plans must be signed before the first payment or reimbursement.
  • Keep records generally at least three years after filing, and longer in some cases.

Why do records matter so much for a tax strategy?

Because a deduction is only as strong as the proof behind it. If the IRS asks about a number on your return, the records are what show the expense was real, was for the business and followed the rules of the strategy you used. That proof is called substantiation.

Strategies that move money between you and your business, like rent, wages to family or reimbursements, draw the most questions. They hold up when the paperwork shows a real arrangement at a fair price, set up before the money moved.

A strategy is only as good as the paper behind it.

What makes a business record hold up?

Records hold up when they were made at the time, tie each cost to a business purpose and show a fair price. These six habits apply to almost every strategy we use.

  1. §1

    Write it down when it happens

    Mileage logs, timesheets and meeting notes made at the time carry far more weight than a reconstruction in March. A two-line note written the same day beats a detailed one written a year later.

  2. §2

    Tie every cost to a business purpose

    Note who, what and why on the receipt or in your books. “Lunch” isn’t a purpose. “Lunch with a client to scope a project” is. IRS Publication 463 explains what the IRS expects for travel, meals, gifts and car expenses.

  3. §3

    Set the rate before you pay it

    Rent to yourself, wages to your kids: pay what the market would pay for the same thing, and keep the comparables that prove it. Fair market value means what a stranger would pay.

  4. §4

    Put the plan in writing first

    Some arrangements, like an accountable plan or an HRA, need a written plan in place before the first reimbursement. Signed late, the same payments can be treated as taxable income.

  5. §5

    Keep business and personal money apart

    Separate accounts make every other record easier to trust. Move money between them on purpose, and label each transfer.

  6. §6

    Keep it long enough

    Keep records at least as long as the IRS can review the return they support. The next section covers how long that usually is.

Which records does each strategy need?

Each strategy has its own paper trail, and most of it has to exist before or as the money moves. Here are the records usually involved for a few strategies we use all the time. Your team will tell you exactly what applies to you.

The Augusta Rule comes from IRC §280A(g), which is why the rent has to match fair market value and the home can be rented for up to 14 days a year. The worksheet exists to prove the rate.

How long should I keep business tax records?

Generally at least three years after you file, and longer in some situations. That’s the usual window the IRS has to review a return, but it can be longer, for example if income was substantially underreported or a return was never filed. Employment tax records and records for property you still own have their own timelines.

The IRS guide on how long to keep records lists the periods by situation. For plan documents like an accountable plan or an HRA, keep them for as long as the plan is in use, plus the review period for the last return that relied on them. When in doubt, ask your team before you shred anything.

What’s a simple record-keeping system that works?

The simplest system is one business account, receipts captured the same day and books closed every month. It doesn’t need special software. It needs a routine.

  • One business account and card. Every business cost goes through it, so the books start clean.
  • Capture receipts the day you get them. A photo with a one-line purpose note is enough for most costs.
  • A folder per strategy. The signed plan, the comparables, the logs and the payments, together.
  • Close the books monthly. Categorized books catch gaps while they’re still easy to fix. Bookkeeping and payroll can run under the same roof as your strategy.
  • A monthly reminder. Ten minutes to check that logs, timesheets and reimbursements are current.
