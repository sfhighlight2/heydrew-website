Proactive tax planning vs. reactive tax prep

Proactive tax planning means making tax moves while the year is still open, then documenting them as you go. Reactive tax prep reports a year that’s already over. The difference is timing: planning early leaves room to change how you’re paid, who you hire and how expenses are reimbursed.

Key takeaways

  • Reactive tax prep reports what already happened. Proactive planning decides what happens next.
  • The levers are ordinary: your pay, who works in the business, reimbursements and savings.
  • Most moves for a tax year need to be in place by December 31, so earlier leaves more options.
  • Proactive isn’t aggressive. It’s the existing rules, used on purpose and documented.

What is reactive tax planning?

Reactive tax planning is tax work that starts after the year is over. You hand over your numbers, a professional reports what already happened, and the return goes out. That work matters: somebody has to file an accurate return. But it looks backward, at a year you can no longer change.

Most business owners only ever get this kind of help. The conversation happens in March or April, when the bill is already mostly decided, and the only real question left is how to pay it.

By the time a return is filed, almost every decision that shaped the bill has already been made.

What is proactive tax planning?

Proactive tax planning means making tax decisions during the year, while they can still change the outcome. You look at how you’re paid, who works in the business, how expenses are handled and what you’re saving, then you adjust the pieces you’re allowed to adjust.

The second half is just as important: you document each move as you make it. Agreements get signed before the money moves. Logs get kept as the work happens. By the time you file, the return is a review of a plan you already followed, not a scramble to reconstruct one.

How do proactive and reactive planning compare?

The difference is timing, and timing decides what you can still change. Here they are side by side.

Reactive tax prep and proactive tax planning, side by side
QuestionReactiveProactive
When the work happensAfter the year endsAll year, starting early
What gets looked atLast year’s numbersThis year’s decisions
Who starts the conversationYou, at filing timeYour team, on a schedule
What you can still changeVery littlePay, hiring, reimbursements, savings
The paperworkRebuilt from memoryKept as it happens

What can you still change during the year?

More than most owners expect, as long as the year is still open. The levers below are ordinary business decisions; planning just makes them on purpose.

How you pay yourself

If your business has elected S-Corp status, you generally take a salary for the work you do and can take remaining profit as distributions. The salary has to be reasonable for the role, and choosing and documenting it is one of the biggest planning decisions for S-Corp owners. It’s much easier to set during the year than to fix after it.

Who works in the business

Paying your children real wages for real work can move income from your tax rate to theirs, if the job, the hours and the pay all hold up. That only works if the work and the payroll happen during the year. Here’s how hiring your kids works, and who it tends to fit.

How expenses are reimbursed

An accountable plan is a written policy that lets your business reimburse business costs you paid personally, with receipts and a business purpose for each. It needs to be adopted before the reimbursements start, which is why it belongs at the start of the year. See how an accountable plan works. A related move, renting your home to your business under the Augusta Rule, depends on meetings that are planned, held and recorded as they happen.

What you save, and where

Retirement and health accounts can lower taxable income, but each has its own contribution windows and setup rules. A Solo 401(k) or an HSA has to be open and funded on time to count for the year, so these decisions get made in the fall at the latest, not in April.

When should I start tax planning?

As early in the year as you can, and certainly before the fourth quarter. Most moves for a tax year need to be in place by December 31, and some need paperwork before the first dollar moves. Starting in January gives you twelve months to act. Starting in November gives you a few weeks.

Starting mid-year is still worth it. You won’t get back the months that passed, but the rest of the year is still open, and a plan that covers half the year is better than none. If you’re also behind on past filings, bring that up early so the catch-up and the plan don’t compete for the same weeks.

Estimated taxes run on their own schedule. If you have income without withholding, the IRS generally expects payments during the year. The IRS page on estimated taxes explains who pays and how; your team can tell you what applies to you.

Does proactive planning mean being aggressive?

No. Proactive planning is about timing and documentation, not risk. The strategies it uses are written into the tax code; planning just makes sure you use the ones you qualify for, in the right way, at the right time.

Proactive doesn’t mean aggressive. It means using the rules that already exist, on purpose, with paperwork that shows you qualified.

Good planning should make your return easier to defend, not harder. A deduction backed by a signed plan, a fair-market rate and records made at the time is stronger than the same deduction pieced together at filing. Our guide on keeping records that hold up covers what that paper looks like.

What does a proactive year look like?

A proactive year follows a simple rhythm: set the plan early, check it as the year moves, and finish the paperwork before December 31. In practice it looks something like this.

  1. 01

    Early in the year

    Set this year’s plan and your salary, adopt any written plans, and start fresh logs and timesheets.

  2. 02

    Spring

    File last year’s returns or extensions, and check the first quarter’s books against the plan.

  3. 03

    Mid-year

    Check whether income is tracking the plan. Adjust pay or distributions if the year changed.

  4. 04

    Fall

    Run a year-end projection and decide on retirement, equipment and other moves while there’s time.

  5. 05

    By December 31

    Run final payroll, submit the last reimbursements and finish the paperwork for this year’s strategies.
