Proactive vs. reactive tax planning

Most tax work is reactive. The year ends, you hand over your numbers, and a professional reports what already happened. That's necessary, but by then almost every decision that shapes your bill has been made.

Proactive planning moves the conversation to the front of the year. You look at how you're paid, who works in the business, how expenses are handled and what you're saving, while there's still time to change them. Then you document the moves as you make them, so filing becomes a review, not a surprise.

Proactive doesn't mean aggressive. It means using the rules that already exist, on purpose, with paperwork that shows you qualified.

How to keep records that hold up

A strategy is only as good as the paper behind it. These habits apply to almost every strategy we use.

  1. §1

    Write it down when it happens

    Mileage logs, timesheets and meeting notes made at the time carry far more weight than a reconstruction in March.

  2. §2

    Tie every cost to a business purpose

    Note who, what and why on the receipt or in your books. "Lunch" isn't a purpose. "Lunch with a client to scope a project" is.

  3. §3

    Set the rate before you pay it

    Rent to yourself, wages to your kids: pay what the market would pay, and keep the comparables that prove it.

  4. §4

    Put the plan in writing first

    Some arrangements, like an Accountable Plan or an HRA, need a written plan in place before the first reimbursement.

  5. §5

    Keep business and personal money apart

    Separate accounts make every other record easier to trust. Move money between them on purpose, and label it.

  6. §6

    Keep it long enough

    The IRS generally has three years after you file to review a return, and longer in some situations. Ask your team how long to keep each type of record.

A year of planning moves, month by month.

General and educational. Deadlines shift with weekends, holidays, extensions and your entity type, so confirm each one with your team.

A filing or payment deadline usually falls this monthA planning move

  1. January

    • Year-end wage and contractor forms go out to workers
    • Last estimated tax payment for the prior year
    • Set this year's plan and salary with your team
    • Start fresh mileage logs and timesheets

  2. February

    • Close out last year's books
    • Gather prior-year documents for filing
    • Confirm reimbursement paperwork is complete

  3. March

    • S-Corp and partnership returns come due before personal returns
    • Decide on extensions with your team
    • Review first-quarter payroll

  4. April

    • Federal individual filing deadline
    • First estimated tax payment for this year
    • Prior-year IRA and HSA contributions can generally still be made up to the filing deadline

  5. May

    • Review first-quarter books against the plan
    • Submit reimbursements with receipts
    • Check that kids' timesheets are current

  6. June

    • Second estimated tax payment
    • Mid-year check-in: is income tracking the plan?
    • Keep agendas and notes for any home meetings

  7. July

    • Adjust salary or distributions if the year changed
    • Summer work for the kids: job descriptions and hours

  8. August

    • Back-to-school: review college savings
    • Check retirement contributions so far

  9. September

    • Third estimated tax payment
    • Extended S-Corp and partnership returns usually come due
    • Start the fourth-quarter projection

  10. October

    • Extended individual returns usually come due
    • Open enrollment: check whether your health plan is HSA-eligible
    • The year-end planning window is open

  11. November

    • Run the year-end projection with your team
    • Decide on equipment and retirement moves
    • Finish any paperwork for this year's strategies

  12. December

    • December 31: most moves for this tax year must be in place
    • Run final payroll, including any kids' wages
    • Submit the last reimbursements of the year

Plain-English glossary.

Accountable Plan
A written policy that lets your business reimburse you for business costs you paid personally. Done right, the business deducts the reimbursement and you don't pay income tax on it.
Augusta Rule
A rule in Section 280A that lets you rent your home for up to 14 days a year without reporting the rent as income. Businesses use it for real meetings, at a fair market rate.
Deduction
An amount that reduces your taxable income. It is not a dollar-for-dollar cut in tax; what it saves depends on your tax rate.
Credit
An amount that reduces your tax bill directly, dollar for dollar. Rarer than deductions, and often with strict rules.
Estimated taxes
Payments made during the year on income that has no withholding, like business profit. They are usually due quarterly.
Fair market value
What a stranger would pay for the same thing. Rent, wages and reimbursements between you and your business should match it.
HRA
Health Reimbursement Arrangement. An employer-funded plan that reimburses qualifying medical costs for eligible employees, under a written plan document.
HSA
Health Savings Account. Available with an HSA-eligible, high-deductible health plan. Contributions can be deductible, and money used for medical costs comes out tax-free.
Hire Your Kids
Paying your children real wages for real work in your business. The business deducts the wages, and the kids may owe little or no income tax on them.
Pass-through entity
A business whose profit "passes through" to the owners' personal returns, like an S-Corp, partnership or single-member LLC.
Proactive planning
Making tax decisions during the year, while they can still change the outcome, instead of reporting them after the year ends.
Reasonable compensation
The salary an S-Corp owner who works in the business should pay themselves: roughly what the business would pay someone else for the same work.
Roth vs. Traditional IRA
Two retirement accounts. Traditional contributions may be deductible now and taxed later; Roth contributions are taxed now and can come out tax-free later.
S-Corp
A tax election, not a type of company. An LLC or corporation can elect it. Owners who work in the business take a salary, and remaining profit can be paid as distributions.
Solo 401(k)
A retirement plan for a business with no employees besides the owner (and a spouse). You contribute as both employee and employer, which can allow more than an IRA.
Substantiation
The proof behind a number on your return: receipts, logs, agreements and notes that show the expense was real and for business.
529 plan
A savings account for education. Growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified education costs are tax-free. Some states add a deduction.
