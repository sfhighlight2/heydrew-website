Scenario A

The S-Corp consultant

Illustrative scenario, not a client

The situation

Runs a one-person consulting firm taxed as an S-Corp. Works from a dedicated room at home, drives to client sites, and pays for a phone and software personally. Holds a planning day with a contractor each quarter. Files on time, but nobody has planned anything.

Questions the Tax Valet asks

  • How did you set your salary, and when was it last reviewed?
  • Which business costs are you paying personally right now?
  • Is there a room at home used only for the business?
  • Where do your quarterly planning days happen today?
  • What have you put toward retirement so far this year?

Strategies explored

  • Likely fitAccountable PlanThe S-Corp reimburses home office, phone and mileage costs paid personally, as long as they're documented and submitted on time.
  • Worth a lookAugusta RuleRenting the home to the business for real meetings, at a fair market rate, for no more than 14 days a year.
  • Likely fitSolo 401(k)With no employees besides the owner, it may allow larger contributions than an IRA. Timing rules apply.
  • ReviewOwner salaryFor S-Corp owners, a reasonable salary is a strategy decision, not just a payroll setting.

What the plan documents

  • A signed Accountable Plan resolution Get the template
  • Monthly reimbursement reports with receipts and mileage logs
  • A comparable rent worksheet for the home meeting space Get the worksheet
  • An agenda, attendee list and notes for each meeting day
  • Retirement plan paperwork and a contribution schedule

Illustrative scenario. A hypothetical owner built to show the process, not a HeyDrew client. Whether any strategy fits you depends on your facts and the rules in effect for your tax year.

Scenario B

The family business with working kids

Illustrative scenario, not a client

The situation

Married co-owners of a small online shop. Their two teenagers pack orders after school and run the shop's social accounts. The kids get paid now and then, in cash, with no records. College is a few years out.

Questions the Tax Valet asks

  • How old is each child, and what work do they actually do?
  • How many hours a week, and what would you pay someone else for it?
  • How is the business set up for tax: sole proprietorship, partnership or S-Corp?
  • Do the kids have bank accounts in their own names?
  • What are you already saving for college or retirement?

Strategies explored

  • Likely fitHire Your KidsReal wages for real work, paid through payroll and deductible to the business. How payroll taxes apply depends on the entity.
  • Worth a lookTraditional & Roth IRAKids with earned income can contribute to an IRA of their own. Which kind depends on their situation.
  • Worth a look529 Education PlanTax-advantaged college savings. State rules on deductions vary.
  • StopCash with no recordsUndocumented payments don't support a deduction. The plan replaces them.

What the plan documents

  • A written job description for each child
  • Timesheets, kept as the work happens
  • Pay rates compared with what the same work costs elsewhere
  • Payroll records and year-end wage forms
  • Statements showing wages paid into the kids' own accounts

Illustrative scenario. A hypothetical family built to show the process, not a HeyDrew client. Whether any strategy fits you depends on your facts and the rules in effect for your tax year.

Scenario C

The owner who also holds rentals

Illustrative scenario, not a client

The situation

Owns an operating business taxed as an S-Corp and, separately, two long-term rental properties titled in the owners' personal names. The business books are clean. The rentals live in a spreadsheet.

Questions the Tax Valet asks

  • How is each property titled, and is it insured to match?
  • Who manages the rentals, and roughly how many hours go into them?
  • Do you have purchase and improvement records for each property?
  • Is anything being bought, sold or refinanced this year?
  • Does the business ever meet at your home?

Strategies explored

  • FirstProperty recordsClean, property-by-property books and depreciation records come first. Every other decision depends on them.
  • ReviewHow the properties are heldOwnership and titling affect liability, bookkeeping and taxes. Worth reviewing with your team and an attorney.
  • UnlikelyReal estate professional statusIt comes with strict time tests. Alongside a full-time business, most owners won't meet them, and the plan says so.
  • Worth a lookAugusta RuleSeparate from the rentals: the owners' own home, used for real business meetings, at a fair market rate.

What the plan documents

  • A ledger for each property, kept with the bookkeeping
  • Purchase, improvement and depreciation records
  • An hour log, if time spent is part of the decision
  • A comparable rent worksheet for any home meetings Get the worksheet

Illustrative scenario. A hypothetical owner built to show the process, not a HeyDrew client. Whether any strategy fits you depends on your facts and the rules in effect for your tax year.

Scenario D

The solo creator

Illustrative scenario, not a client

The situation

A single-member LLC earning from brand sponsorships, platform payouts and affiliate links. Income arrives in bursts. Buys camera gear and works from a home studio. Has a high-deductible health plan and no retirement account yet.

Questions the Tax Valet asks

  • Where does income arrive, and how is it tracked today?
  • Is the LLC taxed as a sole proprietorship, or has it elected S-Corp status?
  • How are you handling estimated tax payments on uneven income?
  • Is your health plan HSA-eligible?
  • What gear and studio costs did you pay for this year, and do you have receipts?

Strategies explored

  • Likely fitSolo 401(k)Built for owners with no employees. Contributions can flex from year to year, which suits uneven income.
  • Likely fitHealth Savings AccountWith an HSA-eligible plan, contributions can be deductible and grow tax-free for medical costs.
  • Run the mathS-Corp electionIt makes sense at some income levels and not at others. The plan shows the comparison both ways.
  • DependsAccountable PlanRelevant if the business becomes an S-Corp. As a sole proprietorship, those costs are deducted directly.

What the plan documents

  • Bookkeeping categories for each income source
  • A quarterly estimated tax schedule
  • Retirement and HSA account paperwork
  • Receipts and business-use notes for equipment and the studio

Illustrative scenario. A hypothetical owner built to show the process, not a HeyDrew client. Whether any strategy fits you depends on your facts and the rules in effect for your tax year.
